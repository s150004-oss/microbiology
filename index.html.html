B. The Domain Bacteria

  • Contains some of the best-studied prokaryotes; classified into several major phyla – about 18 phyla identified from culturing or nucleotide sequencing.
  • Adapted to diverse environments: air, soil, water, and in enormous numbers on the surfaces of virtually all plants and animals. Isolated from Arctic ice, thermal hot springs, the fringes of space, and animal tissues.

C. The Domain Archaea – many extremophiles

Extremophiles grow best at environmental extremes: very high temperatures, high salt concentrations, or extremes of pH. Many live deep in oceans, though some genera thrive under modest conditions. Archaeal genera fit into two major phyla.

A. Bacillus (rod-shaped)

  • Plural bacilli; cylindrical cell from 0.5 µm to 20 µm long.
  • Slender: Salmonella typhi (typhoid fever) · Rectangular with squared ends: Bacillus anthracis (anthrax) · Club-shaped: Corynebacterium diphtheriae (diphtheria).
  • Most occur singly; some form a long chain = streptobacillus (strepto = chains).
"Bacillus" has two meanings: (1) a rod-shaped cell, and (2) a genus name (Bacillus).

B. Coccus (spherical)

Plural cocci (kokkos = "berry"). Quite small: 0.5–1.0 µm diameter. Usually round but may be oval, elongated or indented. Many stay together after division and form characteristic arrangements.

Spiral cells are motile (flagella or axial filaments) and tend to separate after division – use this as your own reasoning, and check your instructor's answer.

4.3 An Overview to Prokaryotic Cell Structure

Cell organization is centered on three specific processes:

1. Sensing & responding to the exteriorMost cells have a cell wall → "external structures" needed to sense the environment and respond to it or to other cells.
2. Compartmentation of metabolismMetabolism must be segregated from the exterior yet able to transport materials to and from it (the cell envelope).
3. Growth & reproductionComplex metabolism occurs in the aqueous cytoplasm; these processes exist as internal structures / subcompartments localized to areas of the cytoplasm.

A. Pili – protein fibers extending from the surface

  • Numerous short, thin, proteinaceous fibers (sing. pilus = "hair") on the surface of most gram-negative bacteria.
  • Fibers are scaffolding for specific adhesive molecules called adhesins.
  • Function: attach cells to surfaces to form biofilms, or (in pathogens) to form microcolonies on human cell and tissue surfaces.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae adhesins anchor to the urogenital tract; Bordetella pertussis (whooping cough) adhesins bind the upper respiratory tract.
  • Conjugation pili: contact between appropriate cells → transfer of genetic material from donor to recipient (conjugation).
  • Also present on some gram-positive species: Corynebacterium diphtheriae, group B Streptococcus.
  • Pili = fimbriae (sing. fimbria = "fibers") – used interchangeably.

B. Flagellar motility

  • Flagellum (pl. flagella) = a remarkable "nanomachine". One or more flagella at one or both ends of the cell (depending on species).
  • Length 10–20 µm (many times the cell diameter); only about 20 nm thick.
Structure in Bacteria
  • Filament – hollow, long rigid protein strands
  • Hook – attaches filament to basal body
  • Basal body – anchored in cell membrane & wall; central rod + enclosing rings
  • Gram-positive: 1 ring in membrane + 1 ring in wall (2 total)
  • Gram-negative: a pair of rings in membrane + a pair in wall (4 total)
Energy & Archaea
  • Rotation energy from diffusion of protons (H⁺) into the cell through proteins associated with the basal body
  • Up to 1,500 rpm → drives the cell forward
  • Archaea: flagellar protein composition & structure differ from Bacteria; motility appears similar
Chemotaxis – behavioral response to chemicals
Flagellar rotationCell movementNotes
Counterclockwise"Run" – straight ahead in short burstsDefault state when there is no gradient
Clockwise"Tumble" – random reorientation for ~1 secondThen the motor reverses and a new run starts in a new direction
With an attractant (e.g., sugar): longer runs + fewer tumbles → biased movement up the concentration gradient (toward the attractant).
  • Similar behaviors: phototaxis (toward light, photosynthetic prokaryotes) and aerotaxis (toward oxygen).

C. Other types of motility

Endoflagella / axial filaments (spirochetes)
  • Flagella extend from one or both poles but fold back along the cell body
  • Endoflagella + cell body are surrounded by an outer sheath membrane
  • Motility from torsion generated by flagellar rotation
  • Less regular and more jerky than flagellar motility
Gliding motility
  • Movement without flagella across a solid surface
  • Along the long axis of bacillus- or filamentous-shaped cells
  • Usually slower than flagellar motility
  • Examples: cyanobacteria and myxobacteria

D. The glycocalyx

Most prokaryotes secrete an adhering layer of polysaccharides (or polysaccharides + small proteins) = glycocalyx. Colonies with a glycocalyx look moist and glistening.

TypeDescription
CapsuleLayer is thick and covalently bound to the cell
Slime layerThinner, diffuse polysaccharides; usually a mass of tangled fibers of dextran that attach the cell to tissue surfaces
Functions
  • Buffer between cell and environment
  • Protects from desiccation (high water content)
  • Attachment to surfaces – e.g., V. cholerae to the intestinal wall
  • Evades the immune system – Streptococcus pneumoniae (bacterial pneumonia) and Bacillus anthracis can't be easily engulfed by white blood cells (phagocytosis)
Biofilms & food spoilage
  • S. mutans → dental plaque = a biofilm on the tooth
  • Biofilm: carbohydrate matrix binds microcolonies; water channels deliver nutrients & remove waste
  • Alcaligenes viscolactis slime → "ropy milk" (thick, stringy)
  • Bread can become ropy if contaminated with slime-producing Bacillus subtilis

4.5 The Cell Envelope

The cell envelope = cell wall + cell membrane. The wall is relatively porous; the membrane regulates transport of nutrients and metabolic products.

Archaeal wall
  • No peptidoglycan

A. Functions of the cell wall

  • Acts as an exoskeleton – protects the cell from injury and damage.
  • With the cytoskeleton, maintains cell shape and reinforces the envelope against osmotic pressure.
  • Most microbes live in a hypertonic-inside condition (more dissolved material inside than outside) → water diffuses inward → osmotic pressure. Without a wall the cell would rupture (lysis).

B. Peptidoglycan (murein) – the bacterial wall polymer

  • Chains of disaccharides (glycan strands) cross-linked by peptide bridges.
  • Each disaccharide = NAG (N-acetylglucosamine) + NAM (N-acetylmuramic acid).
  • The carbohydrate backbone can occur in multiple layers connected by side chains of four amino acids and peptide cross-bridges.

C. Gram-positive vs Gram-negative walls

FeatureGram-positiveGram-negative
PeptidoglycanVery thick (~25 nm), multiple layers cross-linked within and between layersThin, two-dimensional – a single layer, cross-linked only between strands in that layer
Teichoic acidPresent (anionic polysaccharide derivative; bound to NAM)Absent
Outer membraneNoYes (unique feature)
Periplasmic space—Gap between outer and cell membrane; gel-like; contains digestive enzymes & transport proteins; peptidoglycan lies here
Gram stainRetains crystal violet (thick wall)—
Resistance to antimicrobials—More resistant to antimicrobial agents, dyes, disinfectants, lysozyme (porins block large molecules)
Teichoic acids (gram-positive)
  • Wall teichoic acids are bound to NAM
  • Essential for viability – deleting synthesis genes → cell death
  • Function still unclear; may maintain surface charge, control autolytic enzymes, and/or maintain permeability of the wall
Gram-negative outer membrane
  • Inner half: phospholipids (like the cell membrane)
  • Outer half: lipopolysaccharide (LPS) = O-polysaccharide + lipid A
  • O-polysaccharide identifies variants (e.g., strain O157:H7 of E. coli)
  • Lipid A is released on cell death → endotoxin, toxic if ingested (Chapter 18)
  • Porins: proteins forming pores for small molecules; larger molecules can't pass

D. The archaeal cell wall

  • No peptidoglycan → cells are resistant to lysozyme and penicillin.
  • Some have pseudopeptidoglycan: NAM replaced by NAT (N-acetyltalosamine uronic acid).
  • Others: walls of polysaccharide, protein, or both.
  • Most common: the S-layer – hexagonal pattern of protein or glycoprotein self-assembling into a crystalline lattice 5–25 nm thick.
  • Function is the same as in bacteria: mechanical support and prevention of osmotic lysis.

E. The cell (plasma) membrane

Structure – fluid mosaic model
  • Universal structure separating external from cytoplasmic environment; only water diffuses slowly across (small, uncharged)
  • Bacterial membrane: ~5 nm thick, 40% phospholipid + 60% protein
  • Fluid – consistency of olive oil; lipids & proteins move laterally → fluid mosaic model
  • Phospholipid bilayer: charged phosphate hydrophilic head + two hydrophobic fatty-acid tails; tails form the permeability barrier
Disruption of the membrane
  • Polymyxin pokes holes in the bilayer
  • Some detergents and alcohols dissolve the bilayer
  • Result: cytoplasm leaks out → cell lysis and death
Membrane proteins – functions
  • Enzymes for cell-wall synthesis or energy metabolism (prokaryotes lack mitochondria → the membrane performs part of that function).
  • Anchor DNA to the membrane during replication.
  • Receptors of chemical information – sense environmental changes and trigger responses.
Transport across the membrane
ProcessEnergy?DirectionDetails
Facilitated diffusion (passive)NoDown the concentration gradient (high → low)Integral membrane proteins facilitate movement
Active transportYesAgainst the gradientMembrane proteins act as "pumps"; establish different solute concentrations inside/outside; also transport large molecules such as proteins that can't freely diffuse
Archaeal membranes differ
  • Lipid tails are attached to glycerol differently (ether-linked vs ester-linked in Bacteria/Eukarya).
  • No typical fatty acid tails – instead repeating five-carbon units form tails longer than fatty acids.
  • Tails of adjacent sides are bonded together → a lipid monolayer rather than a bilayer.

A. Cytoplasm

Enclosed by the cell membrane; the compartment where most growth and metabolism occur. It consists of the cytosol – a semifluid mass of proteins, amino acids, sugars, nucleotides, salts, vitamins, and ions.

B. The nucleoid & genome

  • Nucleoid = diffuse chromosome region; no covering or membrane; a central subcompartment where DNA aggregates and ribosomes are absent.
  • Usually a single chromosome (closed loop of DNA and protein, with few exceptions).
  • Most cells have one copy of each gene → genetically haploid. Nucleoid and chromosome do not undergo mitosis, and with one gene set they cannot undergo meiosis.
  • Genome = complete set of genes. E. coli (mid-size range): about 4,300 genes → some 2,000 different proteins. Genes determine which proteins/enzymes (and metabolic reactions) the cell can make.

C. Plasmids

  • Smaller DNA molecules; about one-tenth the size of the chromosome; stable, extrachromosomal closed loops with 5 to 100 genes.
  • One or more per cell, same or different genes. Contain nonessential genetic information.
  • Replicate independently of the chromosome; can be transferred between cells during recombination.
  • Important "vectors" in industrial genetic engineering.
  • Some carry genes for disease-causing toxins; many carry genes for chemical or antibiotic resistance → R plasmids (R = resistance).

D. Ribosomes

  • Hundreds of thousands of nearly spherical particles → granular appearance in electron microscope.
  • Size measured by settling rate in a centrifuge – Svedberg units (S). Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S = 30S small subunit + 50S large subunit (built from RNA and protein).
  • Antibiotics such as streptomycin and tetracycline prevent prokaryotic ribosomes from carrying out protein synthesis.

E. Inclusion bodies & other compartments

StructureContents / function
Inclusion bodiesStore nutrients or monomers for cellular structures. Examples: granules of glycogen (polysaccharide), globules of elemental sulfur, lipid. Some help identify pathogens.
Metachromatic granules (volutin)Deposits of polyphosphate (long chains of inorganic phosphate) – e.g., in diphtheria bacilli

F. The prokaryotic "cytoskeleton"

Tubulin homolog
The first prokaryotic cytoskeletal protein discovered. Forms filaments similar to microtubules, but they do not assemble into microtubules.
Actin homologs
Very similar 3-D structure to eukaryotic actin. Filaments help determine cell shape in E. coli and B. subtilis; involved in chromosome segregation during division and magnetosome formation.
Crescentin
Determines the crescent shape of Caulobacter crescentus. In older, filamentous cells it maintains the helical shape by aligning with the inner cell curvature beneath the cytoplasmic membrane.

5.1 Prokaryotic Reproduction

Mind map – Reproduction
Binary fission
Steps
  • Cell doubles its mass
  • DNA replicates & separates
  • Cytokinesis at midcell
  • 2 identical daughter cells
Cytokinesis types
  • Gram-negative: constriction

Generation time
  • S. aureus 30 min
  • M. tuberculosis ~15 h
  • T. pallidum 33 h
  • Growth in microbiology = increase in the number of individuals (population size); each cell carries identical genetic instructions of the parent.
  • Asexual reproduction maintains genetic constancy while increasing cell numbers.
  • Most prokaryotes reproduce by binary fission, usually after a period of growth where the cell doubles in mass: chromosome replicates → the two DNA molecules separate.
  • Prokaryotes lack a mitotic spindle; segregation involves specialized chromosome-associated proteins (how they ensure accurate segregation is not clearly understood).
  • Cytokinesis at midcell = inward pinching of the cell envelope (membrane + wall) → two genetically identical daughter cells.
Cell typeHow cytokinesis occursExample
Gram-negativeConstriction of the cell envelope, followed by cell separationEscherichia coli
Gram-positiveConstriction lets a newly synthesized wall form a septum between daughter cells; separation occurs by dissolution of the septum (slow dissolution → chains of cells)Bacillus licheniformis
Binary fission seems to confer "immortality": there is never a moment when the first bacterial cell has died – each mother cell becomes two young daughter cells.

Generation time (doubling time)

The interval between successive binary fissions of a cell or population. Under optimal conditions some are very fast, others much slower.

SpeciesOptimal generation time
Escherichia coli15 minutes
Staphylococcus aureusabout 30 minutes
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB)approx. 15 hours
Treponema pallidum (syphilis)33 hours
  • If E. coli kept dividing at 15 minutes for 36 hours, the cells would cover the Earth's surface – it doesn't happen because of nutrient limitation and loss of ideal physical factors.
  • Generation time helps determine when disease symptoms appear: faster division → shorter incubation period.
  • Figure example: E. coli from 1 cell to 2 million cells in 7 hours – a J-shaped growth curve, halted only by depletion of food, waste buildup or another limitation.

A. The bacterial growth curve – 4 phases

PhaseWhat happensExtra facts
A. LagNo cell divisions; cells adapt to the new environment – grow in size, store nutrients, synthesize enzymes in preparation for fission.Length depends on metabolic activity of the remaining cells.
B. Logarithmic (log)Exponential growth: all cells undergo binary fission; numbers double each generation; straight line on a log scale.Disease symptoms usually develop here (tissue damage – coughing, fever). Broth becomes turbid; on solid medium visible colonies appear (millions of cells each). Highest antibiotic vulnerability (antibiotics affect actively metabolizing cells).
C. StationaryReproductive and death rates equalize → plateau.Nutrients become scarce, wastes accumulate, O₂ may be short → many cells die.
D. Decline (death)Dying cells far exceed new cells (exponential death phase).Occurs if nutrients stay limited/exceedingly low. Some species (soil bacteria) escape via endospores.

B. Endospores

  • Produced by a few gram-positive species, especially soil bacteria of the genera Bacillus and Clostridium – highly resistant structures (endospores / spores).
  • Normally cells grow, mature and reproduce as vegetative cells. When carbon or nitrogen is limiting and population density reaches a critical mass → stationary phase and sporulation.
1Chromosome replicates; asymmetric cell division
2Smaller cell = prespore (→ endospore); larger = mother cell
3Prespore contains cytoplasm, DNA and lots of dipicolinic acid (stabilizes proteins & DNA)
4Thick peptidoglycan cortex, then protein coats
5Mother cell lyses → mature dormant endospore released
  • Position in the cell can be terminal (end), subterminal (near the end) or central – used for identification.
Resistance (dormant, few chemical reactions)
  • Heat resistant – viable in boiling water (100°C) for 2 hours
  • Survived 20 years in 70% ethyl alcohol
  • Radiation: humans barely withstand 500 rems; endospores survive 1 million rems
  • Can survive extremely long periods
  • Germination: when conditions are favorable the protective layers break down and the spore becomes a vegetative cell
Killing endospores
  • Heating for many hours under high pressure
  • On contaminated machinery (2001 mail sorters): potent but highly dangerous chemical methods (Chapter 23)
  • Exposed postal workers: antibiotics kill newly germinated endospores before vegetative cells make and secrete toxins
  • Anthrax – Bacillus anthracis (studied by Koch and Pasteur); agent of the 2001 bioterror attack by mail. Inhaled spores germinate in the lower respiratory tract; vegetative cells secrete three deadly toxins.
  • Botulism, gas gangrene, tetanus – different species of Clostridium. Clostridial endospores are common in soil and in human/animal intestines; the environment must be oxygen-free for spores to germinate.

HyperthermophilesOptimum >80°CMany are Archaea. High pressure keeps water from boiling → some grow at 113°C.
Psychrotrophic / psychrotolerant species survive at 0°C but prefer to grow at typical mesophile temperatures.

3) pH
  • Most species grow best at neutral pH; human blood/tissues pH 7.2–7.4 suit many pathogens.
  • Growth range usually covers about three pH units. Vibrio cholerae is hardy: pH 2.0 to 9.5.
  • Acidophiles (acid-tolerant) are valuable in food/dairy: Lactobacillus and Streptococcus make acid that converts milk → buttermilk and cream → sour cream. Acidophiles also exist among Archaea.
  • Most bacteria do not grow well in acid → the stomach acid deters disease; acidic foods (lemons, oranges, citrus, tomatoes, many vegetables) are rarely contaminated.
4) Hydrostatic pressure
  • Deep-ocean psychrophiles are under extreme pressure – up to 16,000 psi in deep trenches. Barophiles die quickly at normal atmospheric pressure; prokaryotes may be the only organisms that withstand it.
5) Osmotic pressure / salt & sugar
  • High salt or sugar prevents growth and may kill cells → principle of salting meats and using sugar in jams/jellies.
  • Halophiles need relatively high NaCl: marine prokaryotes thrive in 3.5% salt; some mesophiles are salt-tolerant (e.g., Staphylococcus); extreme halophiles (Archaea) tolerate 20–30% salt.

C. VBNC – viable but non-culturable

  • Fewer than 1% of microorganisms in natural water and soil samples can be cultured → culturability alone can't estimate microbial diversity.
  • Identification: direct microscopic examination and, most commonly, amplification of diagnostic gene sequences or 16S rRNA sequences.
  • Why non-culturable? Some have metabolic injuries (damaged membrane or ribosomes); more often it's simply lack of knowledge about nutritional requirements. Most rickettsiae and chlamydiae grow only in mammalian cell cultures or animals.
  • VBNC studies matter for detecting human pathogens and revealing the diversity of Bacteria and Archaea.

D. Pure cultures – isolation methods

A pure culture contains only one species.

Pour-plate method
A sample of mixed culture is diluted in several tubes of cooled, still-molten agar → poured into sterile Petri dishes and hardened → cells form discrete colonies where most diluted.
Streak-plate method
Single plate of nutrient agar. A sterile loop picks an inoculum from the mixed culture, streaked near one edge; successive streaks thin the cells; incubate → well-isolated colonies = successful isolation.

Chapter 4

Which phylum is the largest and most diverse, and what organelle likely came from its ancestor?
Proteobacteria; the mitochondria of Eukarya (via endosymbiosis).
Which organelle likely came from a cyanobacterial ancestor?
Chloroplasts.
Name the three archaeal groups in Euryarchaeota and one feature of each.
Methanogens (killed by O₂, produce methane), extreme halophiles (need O₂ and high NaCl, pink pigments), thermoacidophiles (high temperature + very low pH).
Which cell wall components are found in Archaea instead of peptidoglycan?
Pseudopeptidoglycan (NAT instead of NAM), polysaccharide, protein, or an S-layer.
Differentiate vibrio, spirillum and spirochete.
Vibrio: comma-shaped curved rod. Spirillum: helical, thick rigid wall, flagella. Spirochete: thin flexible wall, axial filaments (contraction) – e.g., Treponema pallidum.
What is the difference between a capsule and a slime layer?
Capsule = thick, covalently bound to the cell. Slime layer = thinner, diffuse (often dextran).
What drives flagellar rotation, and how fast can it spin?
Diffusion of H⁺ (protons) into the cell through basal-body proteins; up to 1,500 rpm.
Counterclockwise vs clockwise flagellar rotation?
Counterclockwise = run; clockwise = tumble. Toward an attractant: longer runs, fewer tumbles.
What is lipid A and why is it important?
The lipid part of LPS in the gram-negative outer membrane; released on cell death as an endotoxin.
What are porins?
Outer-membrane proteins forming pores that let small molecules into the periplasmic space.
How does the archaeal membrane differ from a bacterial one?
Ether-linked lipids, five-carbon repeating-unit tails, and a lipid monolayer rather than a bilayer.
Why are plasmids called R plasmids?
Many carry genes for resistance to chemicals/antibiotics.
Name the three cytoskeletal proteins/homologs in prokaryotes.
Tubulin homolog, actin homologs, and crescentin.

Chapter 5

Describe the four phases of the growth curve.
Lag (adaptation, no division) → Log (exponential doubling) → Stationary (births = deaths) → Decline (deaths exceed births).
In which phase are cells most vulnerable to antibiotics, and why?
Log phase – antibiotics affect actively metabolizing cells.
Which genera form endospores, and what substance stabilizes proteins and DNA in the prespore?
Bacillus and Clostridium; dipicolinic acid.
Give the four temperature groups and their optimum ranges.
Psychrophiles <15°C; mesophiles 10–45°C; thermophiles ~60°C (40–70°C); hyperthermophiles >80°C.
Which oxygen group needs low O₂ and high CO₂?
Capnophiles (Neisseria, Streptococcus).
Selective vs differential medium – with one example each.
Selective inhibits some organisms (mannitol salt agar for staphylococci). Differential shows biochemical differences with indicators (MacConkey agar).
Which counting method gives viable cells only?
The standard plate count (CFUs).
Why can't we estimate microbial diversity by culturing alone?
Less than 1% of environmental microbes can be cultured (VBNC); methods like 16S rRNA sequencing are used instead.
